A smoke observation can share a test number with another row. An oxygen measurement can belong to a burner or to the surrounding chamber. Follow either count to the original NASA table.

What do the 109 SAME-R observations count?

The CSV has 67 unsuffixed rows and 42 observations with an “a” suffix. Grouping by both material and the numeric part of the label gives 67 groups. Grouping by the number alone gives 66. Kapton 85 and 93 are documented repeats of 75 and retain their own IDs. The crosswalk preserves the original descriptions, dates and aging fields, including empty or unavailable readings. CSV rows 57 and 75 both give test number 57, one for Lampwick and one for Teflon. The reduced-gravity catalogue has a Lampwick 57 folder. Teflon 57 has no corresponding folder, and the catalogue includes Kapton 112 with no matching table row. For four material/number groups, the base and suffixed rows give different dates. A suffix supports grouping by label, while the run identity can remain uncertain.

Where did the 106 extra oxygen matches come from?

At a 34% oxygen cutoff, 113 of the 179 normal-flame attempts meet the condition in the initial chamber gas (column F). In the inverse configuration, 106 of 154 attempts meet the same numerical condition in the burner stream (column H). Pooling them gives 219 apparent matches across 333 attempts. Only 113 have the specified initial chamber measurement.

The query retains nonempty rows across all fuels and ignition outcomes. It tests the original oxygen mole fraction against 0.34, inclusive, before rounding. Inverse CSV rows 18 and 19, for example, specify 85% oxygen in the burner stream and 6% initial ethane in the chamber.
