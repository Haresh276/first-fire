A fire starts in the cabin. What happens next?

Pick a cabin, the item that catches fire and what the crew does. FLINT follows the oxygen, heat, pressure and CO₂ second by second, and shows whether the fire puts itself out before it endangers the crew, and which response works.

The question this answers: in a sealed rover on the Moon, breathing NASA's 34% oxygen exploration atmosphere, does a cabin fire die on its own? If not, should the crew turn the fans off, flood the cabin or vent it, and what does each choice cost them?

    Inside the cabin

    The flame-equivalent oxygen is the dry-air oxygen level that would give the flame the same temperature: the CO₂ and steam the fire makes act like missing oxygen. The fire stops when it falls to the item's limit, or when the item has burned away.

    How the simulator works, and its limits

    • The limit is FLINT's. The least oxygen the item needs comes from FLINT's two-limit model at the cabin's gravity, pressure and fan speed, calibrated on NASA's Earth tests and checked against NASA's simulated-lunar-gravity tests at 8.2 psia with no fan (mean error 1.4 points of oxygen on NASA's summary figure, 2.6 on the individual plots).
    • The fire is a model. A panel lit at its bottom centre grows as a half-ellipse; every patch burns until its fuel is gone. Spread and burning rates grow with the oxygen in excess of the limit; their sizes are assumptions you can change above.
    • The cabin is well mixed. One gas, one temperature; 30% of the heat leaves as radiation and the walls take the rest out slowly. No smoke, carbon monoxide or local hot layers, which matter to a real crew.
    • Crew thresholds. Above 4% CO₂ an unsuited crew must mask up; below 16 kPa of oxygen pressure an unsuited crew is impaired. Floods and venting are the cabin-level responses of FLINT's rover case; pressures far from 8.2 psia are extrapolations.
    • Validated against the Python engine. With sealed-cabin chemistry off it reproduces FLINT's rover case to the half second (acrylic window: 10 kW at 14.5 s, peak 183 kW), and its doses match the engine's to within 0.1%. Not reviewed by NASA; not a safety certification.
