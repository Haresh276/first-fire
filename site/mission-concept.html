Mission concept · after FM²

Four fires that would settle the big questions

Each test below uses hardware NASA already has. For each one, FLINT gives a prediction now, and we state in advance what result would prove FLINT wrong. A model that cannot be proven wrong is not worth flying.

Fire 1 · on the Moon

Read FM² correctly on day one

Question

What is the fabric's real oxygen limit on the Moon?

FM² burns four strips in one sealed 14.4-litre box. As each flame burns, its own CO₂ and steam build up and cool it. So the burns stop sooner than oxygen bookkeeping predicts, and at extinction the oxygen sensor reads higher than the oxygen the flame actually felt. FLINT turns the four measured burn lengths into the limit, with that effect corrected.

FLINT predictsWith lengths read to about 1 cm, the four burns pin the limit to an 80% range about 0.8 points wide (25.6–26.4% if the limit is 26%). At extinction the wet oxygen reading will be about 27.0% against a flame-equivalent 26.0%.

FLINT is wrong if no single oxygen limit can reproduce all four measured burn lengths. The reader runs that consistency check on every result.

Fire 2 · Mars gravity, on Earth

The same drop tower, set to Mars

Question

Do heat-resistant materials get easier to burn on Mars?

NASA measured lunar-gravity limits in a spinning centrifuge inside a drop tower. FLINT says the same rig set to Mars gravity (0.38 g) would show a reversal. The heat-resistant materials, Nomex and polycarbonate, get easier to burn than on the Moon, because the stronger rising-air "fan" stops starving them. Acrylic gets slightly harder to burn.

Material (8.2 psia, no fans)Moon, FLINTMars, FLINTChange

HardwareThe NASA Glenn drop-tower centrifuge used for the lunar tests in ICES-2025-392, run at 0.38 g.
MeasurementsPass or fail of upward spread at a ladder of oxygen levels, as in the lunar series.
Why it mattersMars habitats will use the same materials. Today's rule, passing an Earth test, says nothing about Mars.

FLINT is wrong if Nomex's limit at Mars gravity is not below its measured lunar limit of 25.6%. The predicted drop of 3.8 points is larger than FLINT's model error, so the test is decisive.

Fire 3 · for rover crews

A vented burn at Moon gravity

Question

Can a suited crew put out a cabin fire by letting the air out?

In a pressurized rover at 34% oxygen, FLINT finds that switching the fans off is not enough, and that flooding with CO₂ would need about 26 kg of gas for an acrylic fire, tripling the cabin pressure. Venting is the response a rover has and a space station does not. Below 8.2 psia FLINT is extrapolating, so this test decides it.

HardwareA lunar-gravity centrifuge chamber with a vent valve, or a future FM²-style lander chamber.
ProcedureLight a strip at 8.2 psia and 34% oxygen, then lower the pressure steadily while filming the flame front. Record the pressure at which it goes out.
FLINT predictsNomex goes out near 4.0 psia. Polycarbonate holds to about 1.7 psia, acrylic to about 1.0 psia and the P213 tape to about 0.9 psia.

FLINT is wrong if Nomex keeps burning below 3 psia, or acrylic goes out above 3 psia.

Fire 4 · in freefall

The helium check, on the Space Station

Question

Is helium a safe ingredient for spacecraft air?

FLINT's droplet model, fitted on NASA's FLEX runs, says CO₂ is the better suppressant. Helium can backfire: with heptane fuel the flame survives on less oxygen than in nitrogen. FLEX's own helium runs point the same way, but there are few of them.

Methanol10.7%12.1%11.3%

HardwareThe droplet-combustion apparatus in the Combustion Integrated Rack on the ISS, which ran FLEX.
ProcedureBurn 3 mm heptane droplets in nitrogen and in 30% helium, stepping the oxygen down until the flame dies.

FLINT is wrong if helium raises heptane's limiting oxygen above the nitrogen value.
